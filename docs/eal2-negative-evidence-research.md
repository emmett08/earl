# Negative findings, formal arguments and the likely EAL/2 advantage

**Research synthesis, 23 September 2026.** This note draws predictions from published primary work and the repository's implemented semantics. It reports no new model calls. A bounded optional sampled-negative method and developmental test are [implemented separately](eal2-negative-revision-results.md); the prospective model comparison remains unrun.

## Root claim and the crucial distinction

An EAL/2 argument can make a negative finding consequential when the finding has a formal predicate, usable evidence, a matched scope and a declared relation to the claim. The interpreter can then compute the effect of a new objection or changed evidence on an argument graph. Supplying the same declarations as text to a tool-free model leaves source matching, computation and defeat resolution for that model to perform. The [180-call diagnostic](notation-transfer-live-results.md) found no raw EAL notation advantage over meaning-equivalent JSON on its five exposed tasks. This motivates a checked composition hypothesis; it does not establish how the model internally processed the text.

“Negative finding” covers different inferences:

| Finding | Legitimate conclusion | Required check |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A sampled value violates a bound | The declared sampled predicate failed at that observed point | Match the typed question, scope and in-window witness; missing other samples do not erase a witnessed violation |
| A complete query returns no match | No match occurred **within that certified query scope** | Establish query-relative completeness, source identity, time and search procedure |
| A partial search returns no match | The queried fact remains unknown | Preserve partial coverage; absence of a returned record is insufficient |
| A statistical estimate is not significant | The study did not establish the tested effect | Assess precision against prespecified equivalence bounds before claiming a practically negligible effect |

EAL/2 already permits a successful computational result of `false` to **support an explicitly negative typed proposition**, including `result "holds" == false`; failed execution and incomplete temporal coverage cannot do so ([typed propositions](typed-propositions.md)). Its grounded graph can represent a source-usable objection and an independent defence. An `unsupported` positive claim, however, has no source-usable derivation and is **not** a proof of its negation; rejection in the graph also concerns argument acceptance, not real-world falsity ([argument model](argument-model.md)). A general certificate that an external search exhaustively covers a declared query is not established by these existing predicates. It would need a registered collector or method with independently checked coverage, then a claim whose formal meaning matches that result.

The completeness distinction has a strong empirical basis. Razniewski and Nutt formalise when completeness statements about parts of an incomplete database entail completeness of a particular query. Min and colleagues hold the question and observed facts fixed while changing query-relative coverage. Across three LLM families, complete–partial pairs produce over-closure: models often answer “no” for both the complete and partial member. Structured certificate prompts frequently mischaracterise the evidence's coverage, and prompting shifts over- and under-closure rather than consistently removing them. An EAL/2 assertion of “no result” without checked coverage risks exactly this error. A null statistical result poses another kind of absence problem: Lakens shows how a prespecified equivalence test can assess whether effects larger than a meaningful bound are excluded; an ordinary non-significant test does not perform that assessment.

## Where structured argumentation helps, and where it can fail

Freedman and colleagues' argumentative LLMs construct formal support and attack frameworks. Their contribution to explanation and contestability is direct: another party can challenge an explicit relation and recompute the decision. Their claim-verification accuracy did **not** uniformly exceed direct questioning or chain-of-thought; for GPT-4o on MedClaim, both direct and chain-of-thought scored 0.85, while their argumentative variants scored 0.74–0.83. Exposing an argument graph therefore does not by itself guarantee a better answer.

Fang and colleagues instead combine neural interpretation with an ASPIC+ solver. On BoardGameQA depth three, their Gemini-2 hybrid scored 82.6% against 52.7% for its chain-of-thought comparator; the depth-one comparison was 92.4% versus 93.3%. The advantage grew with defeasible reasoning depth, while the hybrid still made errors when the model triggered or instantiated rules. Their intermediate-format ablation is particularly relevant to EAL authoring: natural-language intermediates scored 92.4%, 87.1% and 82.6% at depths one to three, versus 81.2%, 70.3% and 54.3% for symbolic triples. These are results for their architecture and datasets, not EAL/2 estimates. They caution against assuming that forcing a model to emit a formal representation at every step improves translation. A plausible EAL workflow lets a model propose candidate claims and interpretations, validates the resulting EAL against the original question and sources, and delegates the finite calculation to the interpreter.

Dynamic argumentation research supplies a further reason to test revisions, not just isolated verdicts: adding or retracting an argument or attack can change the accepted extension. EAL/2's named dependencies, scoped evidence and objection targets give an implementation a place to record exactly which relation changed. This is an engineering affordance to test, not a theorem that EAL is faster or more accurate than other representations.

## Discriminating tests

First, hold the queried fact and returned records fixed while changing only a verified coverage assertion: complete and query-covering, partial, then complete for the wrong scope. Measure the proportion of pairs in which the system certifies absence only in the first case, alongside false negative certification and needless abstention. Include a second intervention that adds a genuine observed counterexample to a supported positive claim and checks that an applicable objection changes its status. Vary attack depth and distinguish errors in translating the ordinary brief, checking applicability, computing the graph and reporting the final status.

Second, start from a reviewed argument and successively retract an observation, narrow its covered interval, add a counterexample and add an independent defence. Compare end-to-end correctness, trace completeness, authoring and repair effort, and cost. Blind reviewers should assess whether each formalised claim still states the engineering question. Use held-out briefs and independent reference decisions. A wrong but internally coherent formalisation can pass an interpreter while failing the user's real question.

The strongest rival is a **meaning-equivalent checker** with the same registered computations, graph semantics, provenance checks and host gate, accepting a JSON or library representation. On correctly translated identical inputs it must produce the same verdicts as EAL/2 if both implement the specification. A language-specific advantage therefore has to appear in faithful authoring, detection of malformed or omitted relations, revision correctness, audit trace, integration or resource use under a fair budget. The existing 960-call proposal can diagnose tool-free model behaviour across evidence, notation and prompt length; its sampled counterexample family does not test exhaustive negative licensing, and 960 calls on 24 task roots are not 960 independent engineering problems. No published result guarantees that EAL wins an equal-capability comparison. A tie or loss would narrow the EAL claim while leaving the hybrid improvement over model-only responses open to test.
